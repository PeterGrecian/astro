Peter: *"recent screenshot has orion and M42 circled in blue. I like 1 being
Betelgeuse, maybe we can detect its redness. It would be nice to see how sharp
we can get M42, my first nebula."* Both turn out to be visible in a camera
pointed at the sky over the rooftops: Betelgeuse's colour in a single frame,
and a hint of the nebula after two hours of stacking.

## Betelgeuse's colour

It shows in one frame. Measured from the raw Bayer photosites (each star's 60
second trail, sky subtracted per channel), with Rigel, a near-white star in the
same frame, as the white reference:

| star | blue/green relative to Rigel |
|---|---|
| Rigel | 1.00 |
| Alnitak | 0.77 |
| Alnilam | 1.00 |
| Mintaka | 1.17 |
| Bellatrix | 0.89 |
| Saiph | 0.87 |
| **Betelgeuse** | **0.21** |

The hot blue stars scatter by about 0.2 magnitudes around Rigel. The three
belt stars, which are nearly one colour, show how much one frame wobbles.
Betelgeuse has a fifth of their blue for its green: **1.7 magnitudes redder**,
about eight times the scatter. The catalogue puts the two stars 1.88 apart in
B-V, and the camera's green is broader than V, so this is the size expected.

The red channel does not cooperate yet. The belt stars give red/green of 0.44,
0.06 and 0.03, so in one frame it is measuring something other than the star,
probably the orange sky glow.

## M42

114 one-minute frames from 02:28 to 04:30 UTC, about 1.9 hours, stacked on the
03:13 frame. The camera does not track, so the sword drifts about 12 pixels a
minute and the field slowly turns; each frame is aligned by matching the stars
around the sword, and each colour is resampled separately. Satellite and
aircraft trails drop out of a clipped mean.

In the stack the knot at M42 has a halo out to about 20 pixels, spreading down
and left, which the sword stars above and below it do not have. That is the
nebula's brightest part, the region around the Trapezium.

Not proven in numbers yet. The sword carries about 21% more light than its
stars predict, but the same test on the belt and on empty sky is off by about
10% either way, so the sword's excess is only twice the method's error.

## Nine nights, and undoing the trails

Every star is a short dash because the sky moves during each minute-long
frame. The dash is the same length and direction in every frame (the camera
exposes 59.9 of every 59.9 seconds), so it can be partly undone: tell a
deconvolution the exact smear and it puts the light back where it came from.
On one night the belt stars go from 2.7 times longer than wide to about 1.5.

Then more nights. Orion reaches the same place in the frame about four minutes
earlier each night, and nine nights between 17 September and 9 October were
clear when it did: 665 frames, about 11 hours. Not every minute is equal (haze,
twilight, the odd thin cloud), so each frame counts in proportion to how
clearly it shows the stars, rather than all equally. That alone is worth about
13% more exposure. The nine nights together give 2.2 times the signal to noise
of one night, about five good nights' worth, because several were hazy: the
worst let through only a third of the starlight.

Deconvolved, the deep stack is the clearest picture of M42 so far. The stars
collapse to near points (the belt stars to 1.2 to 1.5 times longer than wide)
and the glow around the Trapezium does not: it stays a spread-out patch,
which is what a nebula should do and a star cannot. The stars are slightly
softer than one night's, because the September nights line up less exactly.

The trails can only be undone so far. The sky always smears east-west, so a
fixed camera can never cross its own trails; what would help is frames of
different lengths, which put the gaps in the smear in different places.

## How faint

The stack also brings up stars far below what one frame shows. One faint
dash just east of the sword measures **magnitude 6.9 +/- 0.3** against the
belt stars. Its position, from a local fit to the belt, M42 and iota Ori,
is 6 arcminutes from **HD 37410**, a white A2 star of catalogue magnitude
**6.86**, and the only star that bright within half a degree. It is invisible
in a single frame and a clear detection (signal to noise about 9) in 1.9
hours: just below the naked-eye limit of a dark site, from suburban London.
