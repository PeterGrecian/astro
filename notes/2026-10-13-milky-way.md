Peter, on first seeing it: *"that is so beautiful."*

## What it is

astrocam is a fixed camera pointed north from a garden in Surbiton, taking a
one-minute exposure all night. It does not track, so the sky turns through
the frames. On 7-8 October, a clear night with no Moon, it took 533 usable
frames.

Each frame was turned about the pole so its stars landed where they were at
00:05 BST, the city's sky glow was subtracted, the trees and the edge of the
roof were masked, and the frames were added. That needs the camera's
geometry known to under a binned pixel across the whole frame (a binned
pixel is a 2 x 2 set of photosites, about 2 arcminutes here), and it is:
stars come out as points from edge to edge.

## What is in it

- **The Milky Way**, the disc of our own galaxy seen edge-on from inside. Here
  it is the autumn stretch, from Cassiopeia down through Perseus, and it
  follows the galactic equator closely.
- **Dark lanes** along the band near Cassiopeia. These are dust clouds in
  the disc blocking the stars behind them.
- **The Double Cluster** in Perseus and the **Alpha Persei cluster** around
  Mirfak, two groups of young stars, both obvious knots.
- Polaris, Kochab in the Little Dipper, Errai in Cepheus, Algol.

## How deep

The faintest stars picked out are about magnitude 9: binocular depth, about
100 times fainter than the faintest star the naked eye sees from here
(about magnitude 4, under a sky of [18.1 magnitudes per square
arcsecond](/astro/notes/2026-09-21-astrocam-sky-brightness)).

Adding 533 frames should have gone over 3 magnitudes deeper than one frame.
It went about 1. The reason is in the picture: at 2 arcminutes a pixel,
every pixel already holds several faint stars too close
together to separate. That grain of unresolved stars is what makes the
Milky Way look like the Milky Way, and it is also the floor. More nights will
not go deeper with this lens; only a longer one will.

## What else shows

- **Faint rings around Polaris**, top left. These are the cover glass, not
  the sky: anything fixed to the camera is swept into circles about the pole
  when the frames are turned. See [Newton's rings on
  astrocam](/astro/notes/2026-10-10-astrocam-rings).
- Stars near the edges are slightly stretched. That is the minute-long
  exposure's own trail, not an error in the geometry.
