Peter, on first seeing it: *"that is so beautiful."*

## What it is

astrocam is a fixed camera pointed north from a garden in Surbiton, taking a
one-minute exposure all night. It does not track, so the sky turns through
the frames. On 7-8 October, a clear night with no Moon, it took 533 usable
frames.

Each frame was turned about the pole so its stars landed where they were at
00:05 BST, the city's sky glow was subtracted, the trees and the edge of the
roof were masked, and the frames were added. That needs the camera's
geometry known to under a binned pixel across the whole frame (a binned
pixel is a 2 x 2 set of photosites, about 2 arcminutes here), and it is:
stars come out as points from edge to edge. Each frame was also spread onto
a grid twice as fine (drizzling), so the picture is at the full resolution of
the sensor, not of the binned pixels the geometry works in.

## When the Milky Way was there

Not all night, on either night. astrocam looks north, and as the night turns, Cassiopeia and
Perseus climb towards the zenith and out of the top of the frame. About 40
degrees of the band were in view from dusk to about 02:15 BST, less by 02:30,
and none from 03:00 until dawn. Early in the evening it was in view but the
sky was 60% brighter (last figure), so it only stands out from about 22:00.

The stack counts each patch of sky only in the frames that actually saw it.
Polaris is in all 533; the Double Cluster in 298, from 20:51 to 01:54 BST;
Mirfak in 207 and Caph in 206. So the Milky Way here is four to five hours of
sky, not nine.

## What is in it

- **The Milky Way**, the disc of our own galaxy seen edge-on from inside. Here
  it is the autumn stretch, from Cassiopeia down through Perseus, and it
  follows the galactic equator closely.
- **Dark lanes** along the band near Cassiopeia. These are dust clouds in
  the disc blocking the stars behind them. The darkest runs just beside
  the middle line of the disc, and it is in the same place on both nights
  while it turned with the stars through each one, so it is the sky and not
  the camera.
- **The Double Cluster** in Perseus and the **Alpha Persei cluster** around
  Mirfak, two groups of young stars, both obvious knots.
- Polaris, Kochab in the Little Dipper, Errai in Cepheus, Algol.

## How deep

The faintest stars picked out are about magnitude 9: binocular depth, about
100 times fainter than the faintest star the naked eye sees from here
(about magnitude 4, under a sky of [18.1 magnitudes per square
arcsecond](/astro/notes/2026-09-21-astrocam-sky-brightness)).

Adding 533 frames should have gone over 3 magnitudes deeper than one frame.
It went about 1. The reason is in the picture: at 2 arcminutes a pixel,
every pixel already holds several faint stars too close
together to separate. That grain of unresolved stars is what makes the
Milky Way look like the Milky Way, and it is also the floor. More nights will
not go deeper with this lens; only a longer one will.

## A second night, with shorter frames

On 9 October astrocam switched from one-minute frames to 15 seconds, and
that night was dark and clear too, so here it is the same way. A star
crosses the sensor while the shutter is open; in a minute it draws a dash,
in 15 seconds a quarter of one. Turning and adding the frames cannot undo
the dash, so the shorter frames give rounder stars.

They also go deeper where it is crowded. Measured against the Gaia
catalogue in the same patches of sky on both nights (magnitude at which
half the catalogue stars are picked out):

| patch | 7-8 Oct, 60 s | 9-10 Oct, 15 s |
|---|---|---|
| Milky Way, Cassiopeia | 7.96 | 8.35 |
| Milky Way, Perseus | 8.14 | 8.59 |
| centre of the frame | 8.97 | 9.00 |
| below the pole | 9.02 | 9.10 |
| near the pole | 9.44 | 8.92 |

In the Milky Way the 15 second night goes about 0.4 magnitudes deeper,
with no more sky time on the band and in Cassiopeia much less. That is
where stars are packed tightest, so a tighter star stands out from its
neighbours. Away from the band it is a draw. Near the pole stars barely
move in a minute, so shorter frames gain nothing there; this night also had
less time overall (7.0 hours against 8.9) and stronger rings from the cover
glass around Polaris.

So shorter frames help, but they do not break the floor: the faintest stars
are still about magnitude 9, set by the size of the pixels.

## What else shows

- **Faint rings around Polaris**, top left. These are the cover glass, not
  the sky: anything fixed to the camera is swept into circles about the pole
  when the frames are turned. See [Newton's rings on
  astrocam](/astro/notes/2026-10-10-astrocam-rings).
- Stars near the edges are slightly stretched. That is the minute-long
  exposure's own trail, not an error in the geometry.
