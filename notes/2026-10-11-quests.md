## The dream

On **25 May 2026 at 22:13** a line went into the astro TODO list: *"Neptune in
November (target)"*. Magnitude 7.8, so well below what the eye can see from a
dark site, never mind a London back garden, and the camera was a Pi with a
wide lens. The budget written that night guessed **about 28 hours** of stacking
to reach it.

A month later, on 25 June, it became the north star: *"positively track Neptune
in the autumn"*. The honest baseline written down that day was **two objects
identified, Polaris and the Moon**.

## The dot

On **10 October**, late in the evening, the whole-night stack of 7 October
showed a faint dot at Neptune's predicted position. That was **138 days** after
the TODO line. The stack was 248 frames of 60 s, about **4 hours**, not 28.
Three things beat the guess: full-resolution raw mosaic frames, a fitted camera model good
to a fraction of a photosite, and fitting every frame's pointing on the stars close
to the planet.

A dot is not a planet until it moves. Four more nights came out the same way,
from 20 September to 7 October. Each time the dot sat within about 1.5
arcminutes of the ephemeris (an eclipticam v3w binned pixel, 2 x 2 photosites as used here, is about
3.5 arcminutes near the centre of the frame). The places it
had left were empty: in the 20 September stack, the spot Neptune would occupy
on 7 October shows nothing (1.1 sigma), and the reverse holds too. No catalogue
star brighter than 7.5 lies within 58 arcminutes.

By the next day the whole season was in: **47 nights** stacked, Neptune found
on **27** of them, and an animation of it walking west through Pisces across
two months.

## Saturn's rings

Saturn's rings are almost edge on and much too small to be seen with these
cameras. FirstScope feasibly could just resolve them, but aiming would be
difficult.

**The next quest** is Neptune again, but properly. Two Raspberry Pi HQ cameras
with a 16 mm lens arrived on 9 October. With them Neptune should show at about
20 sigma in a single 5 second frame. The plan is to tilt the camera on an SG90 servo
to find the ecliptic, and mount the camera in portrait orientation - maybe even tilted perpendicular to the ecliptic so we have a good chance of each planet crossing the field of view.
